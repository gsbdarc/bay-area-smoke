Bay Area Smoke Trends

If you are booking a wedding, a conference, or anything else outdoors months or years out, the question you actually need answered is how likely is smoke on that date, in that place? This is two decades of public air-quality and satellite data, arranged to answer exactly that.

Methods & caveats

Read this before you trust a number

  • A plume overhead is not smoke in your lungs. NOAA's satellite analysts draw a plume as seen from above — a vertical column. The clearest illustration is 9 September 2020, San Francisco's orange-sky day: unforgettable overhead, but comparatively modest ground-level PM2.5. Smoke is only ever attributed here when a plume overhead coincides with a measured surface anomaly.
  • Smoke PM2.5 is an attribution, not a measurement. It is the PM2.5 above a local, seasonal, non-smoke baseline on days a plume was overhead. It absorbs any other anomaly that happened to coincide, and it reports exactly zero when the satellite saw no plume — even if smoke was in fact present.
  • Past frequency is not a forecast. A 35% historical rate on 14 September is a base rate from around twenty observations, not a probability for next year. Fire regimes are changing faster than the record can track.
  • Missing data is not clean air. A year only counts toward a location's statistic if that location actually had working instrumentation that year. Without that rule Napa — which lost its monitor in 2021 — would look like the safest place in the region.

Where the numbers come from

How smoke is attributed

For the years ECHOLab has not published, we reimplement the station-level half of Childs et al. (2022): build a complete location × day panel; mark a day as smoke if any HMS plume polygon covers the point; take the background as the median PM2.5 over non-smoke days in the same location and calendar month, pooled across the year before, the year itself, and the year after; and set smoke PM2.5 to the positive part of the difference on smoke days, and exactly zero otherwise.

Two deliberate deviations, both of which make our numbers slightly less good than the published ones. The three-year background window is centered, which is impossible for the current year, so from the current year onward we use a trailing window and label those values provisional. And we skip the HYSPLIT/AOD gap-filling and the gradient-boosted spatial interpolation entirely — we need point estimates at ten places, not a national grid.

Known holes in the monitoring network

  • Napa is measured from January 2007 to 20 May 2021 by two monitors 4 km apart (06-055-0003, then 06-055-0004), stitched into one series. It has had no EPA PM2.5 monitor since. Before 2007 and after 2021 the smoke line is the modelled grid and total PM2.5 is simply absent.
  • Half Moon Bay has never had one — there is no EPA site anywhere on the San Mateo coast. Its entire series is modelled.
  • Santa Rosa has had no PM2.5 monitor since its 5th St site closed in December 2013; Sebastopol, about 12 km southwest, took over in 2014 and is what this series uses.
  • Livermore's monitor was replaced at the end of January 2024 (06-001-0007 → 06-001-0016, 1.2 km away). The two are stitched into one series; without that the inland East Bay line simply stops.
  • Point Reyes reports under EPA parameter 88502, not 88101. Pull only 88101 and the region's best coastal background site vanishes.
  • Most of the Bay Area stops in 2024, but the edge is moving. BAAQMD's 2025 data reaches EPA slowly and site by site — for a long time none of it was there, in either the bulk files or the API, and it is now arriving piecemeal. Sites run by other agencies (Point Reyes, Santa Cruz) were never affected. Rather than trust this sentence, read the Measured PM2.5 column in the table above: it is generated from the data on every build, so it is always the real answer.
